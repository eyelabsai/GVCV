01

The approach

The right procedure for one eye is the wrong one for another.

Clarity in vision. Confidence in life. How we see shapes how we live — and when people see better, they live more fully. That belief is why I chose refractive surgery.

I trained in refractive surgery at Parkhurst NuVision under Dr. Gregory Parkhurst — principal investigator of the FDA EVO ICL trial and one of the country's highest-volume EVO ICL surgeons — and served as ophthalmology chief resident at the University of Missouri. That path gave me high-volume exposure across the whole field: EVO ICL, LASIK, PRK, SMILE, refractive lens exchange, and premium cataract surgery.

I offer all of them, because no two eyes are alike and the best outcome comes from matching the right procedure to the right eye — not from fitting every patient to a single operation. My work is built on education and radical transparency: I want every patient fully informed about their candidacy, their options, the real risks, and the realistic result. For excellent candidates, that understanding becomes excitement. For those who aren't ready, the honest answer is to wait.

I am also, by training, an engineer. Where most surgeons stop at the formula the vendor hands them, I rebuild the formula. The result is a body of work — published research and software used by other surgeons — aimed at one thing: making vision correction more precise, more predictable, and more personal to each individual eye.

The future of vision correction is not a better guess. It is a measurement — precise, data-driven, and personalized to the eye in front of you.

02

What I do

Six procedures. The art is knowing which one.

Phakic IOL

EVO ICL

An implantable collamer lens. No corneal tissue is removed and the lens can be removed again. Often the right answer for strong prescriptions and thinner corneas — and the focus of my research.

Corneal laser

LASIK

The longstanding, ever-improving technique for a reason: fast, predictable, and genuinely excellent within the range of suitable eyes.

Corneal laser

SMILE

Flapless correction through a small incision, extracting a lenticule with a femtosecond laser — a good fit for the right corneas.

Surface ablation

PRK

No flap and a slower recovery — the proper choice for certain corneas, occupations, and enhancement techniques.

Lens-based

Refractive lens exchange

Replaces the eye's aging natural lens to address both reading and distance vision — and removes the possibility of a cataract later.

Lens-based

Cataract surgery

Once the natural lens clouds, cataract surgery with a premium IOL restores clarity while optimizing the visual result to the patient's goals.

Platforms: EVO ICL & EVO+ · excimer and femtosecond laser vision correction · premium IOLs (monofocal, toric, multifocal, EDOF, and light-adjustable).

04

AI research

Training and evaluating frontier AI.

Outside of medicine, I work with frontier AI labs and AI startups on reinforcement learning and the evaluation of large language models.

I design and build reinforcement-learning environments and evaluation harnesses that train and measure frontier models — spanning agentic coding agents, tool use, and reasoning. The environments pose real, verifiable tasks in sandboxed execution, with automated reward and verification (RLHF and reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards), so models can be trained and benchmarked at scale against ground truth rather than vibes.

The tasks reach deep into ML systems and GPU/TPU engineering — CUDA, Triton and Pallas kernels, distributed training (FSDP, ZeRO, NCCL collectives), optimizer internals, and high-throughput inference (vLLM) — alongside full-stack software engineering and infrastructure (Kubernetes, Terraform).

One thread of that work is the one where my two worlds meet: the evaluation of medical AI. As a physician, I build rubric-based health benchmarks in which frontier models answer realistic, multi-turn, multilingual conversations — across layperson and clinician personas, medical specialties, and contexts — and are then graded against physician-written rubric criteria, each carrying a weighted point value that reflects how much it truly matters.

A good health evaluation has to be meaningful, trustworthy, and unsaturated: scores should track real-world impact, faithfully reflect expert clinical judgment, and still leave today's best models room to improve. That means grading not only for accuracy, but for completeness, communication quality, context awareness, and instruction following — and measuring the behaviors that matter most in medicine: emergency triage and referral, responding safely under uncertainty, appropriate hedging, and seeking context for underspecified questions.

I care about the parts that separate a benchmark that looks good from one that is true: model-based graders validated against physician consensus (meta-evaluation), adversarial and synthetic case generation, physician baselines for reference responses, worst-case (worst-of-n) reliability, and the cost-and-accessibility frontier that decides whether better models actually reach low-resource settings.

It is the same instinct that drives my clinical work: the hardest problems are prediction and measurement problems, and the way you improve them is to measure them honestly against the standard of an expert.
